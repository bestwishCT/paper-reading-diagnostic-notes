13: We present ALCE, the first reproducible bench-
14: mark for automatically evaluating LLMs’ gener-
15: ations with citations. ALCE assumes a natural-
16: language question and a retrieval corpus, and re-
17: quires building end-to-end systems to retrieve rel-
18: evant passages from the corpus, generate a re-
19: sponse to the question, and cite corresponding sup-
20: porting passages. We compile three datasets that
21: cover different types of questions and corpora—
22: ASQA (Stelmakh et al., 2022), QAMPARI (Ru-
23: bin et al., 2022), and ELI5 (Fan et al., 2019)—as
24: shown in Table 1. Different from previous bench-
25: marks (Lee et al., 2019; Bohnet et al., 2022), ALCE
26: evaluates long-text generation, focusing on auto-
27: matically evaluating citation quality, and allows
28: citing multiple passages for individual statements.
29: We design automatic evaluation methods in three
30: dimensions: fluency, correctness, and citation
31: quality. Specifically, we use MAUVE (Pillutla
32: et al., 2021) to measure fluency, propose tailored
33: correctness metrics for each dataset, and adopt a
34: natural language inference (NLI) model (Honovich
35: et al., 2022) to measure citation quality. We show-
36: case how the three dimensions together contribute
37: to a robust evaluation, preventing systems from ex-
38: ploiting shortcuts. Additionally, we conduct human
39: evaluation and demonstrate a strong correlation
40: with our automatic metrics.
41: We experiment on multiple systems with state-
42: of-the-art LLMs and retrievers and also propose
43: novel prompting strategies to synthesize retrieved
44: text into text generation. Although all systems are
45: capable of providing fluent and coherent responses,
46: there remains substantial room for improvement in
47: terms of correctness and citation quality: For exam-
48: ple, on the ELI5 dataset, around 50% generations
49: of our ChatGPT and GPT-4 baselines are not fully
50: supported by the cited passages. Additionally, we
51: find that (1) a closed-book model (generating an-
52: swers without accessing any retrieved documents)
53: with post-hoc citing achieves good correctness but
54: much worse citation quality; (2) although interac-
55: tive retrieval approaches (Yao et al., 2023; Schick
56: et al., 2023) offer more flexibility in when/what
57: to retrieve, they do not improve the performance
58: on this challenging benchmark; (3) summarizing
59: the retrieved passages in a shorter text improves
60: correctness but not citation quality; (4) reranking
61: multiple generations boosts citation quality mea-
62: sured by human evaluation; (5) incorporating more
63: retrieved passages in context does not help Chat-
64: GPT but improves GPT-4 performance.
65: Our extensive analyses highlight three major
66: challenges of building LLMs to generate text with
67: citations: (1) the retrieval quality is crucial to the
68: final performance and has substantial room for im-
69: provement; (2) LLMs’ limited context window re-
70: stricts the number of passages they can incorporate;
71: (3) current LLMs struggle to synthesize multiple
72: documents in context without being distracted by
73: irrelevant ones, although better instruction tuning
74: brings significant improvement. These challenges
75: pose promising research directions for developing
76: better systems integrating retrieval and LLMs.

51: 3 Automatic Evaluation
52: Our benchmark measures the following three di-
53: mensions of system responses:
54: • Fluency: whether the model’s generated text is
55: fluent and coherent.
56: • Correctness: whether the answer is accurate
57: and covers all aspects of interest.
58: • Citation quality: whether the answer is well
59: supported by the cited passages and no irrelevant
60: passages are cited.
61: In the following, we present automatic metrics for
62: each dimension and discuss why the combination
63: of the three metrics provides a robust evaluation.
64: 3.1 Fluency
65: We use MAUVE (Pillutla et al., 2021) to evaluate
66: the fluency of the output (§C). We deploy MAUVE
67: for ASQA and ELI5 and omit it for QAMPARI, as
68: QAMPARI only requires a list of short answers as
69: the response and LLMs consistently adhere to the
70: format in our experiments. As MAUVE is sensitive
71: to output length and text style, and most LLMs are
72: capable of producing fluent text, we mainly employ
73: it as a sanity check as long as the MAUVE scores
74: are high enough.
75: 3.2 Correctness
76: Our objective is to measure the informativeness and
77: utility of the generation to the question. Liu et al.
78: (2023) propose to directly evaluateperceived utility
79: by humans, a process difficult to automate. There-
80: fore, we use correctness—whether the response is
81: accurate compared to a ground truth answer—as a
82: proxy. Evaluating the correctness of long-form gen-
83: eration is a challenging task (Krishna et al., 2021),
84: and we describe our strategy for each dataset be-
85: low. Figure 2 illustrates the metrics and we include
86: additional implementation details in §C.
87: For ASQA, we follow Stelmakh et al. (2022)
88: and calculate the recall of correct short answers by
89: checking whether the short answers (provided by
90: the dataset) are exact substrings of the generation
91: (exact match recall; EM recall).
92: For QAMPARI, we follow Rubin et al. (2022)
93: and calculate the precision and recall of the model
94: prediction, by checking the exact match to the gold
95: answer list. We add one additional adjustment:
96: considering that users often want to know only a
97: few example answers of the question, our evalua-
98: tion considers recall to be 100% if the prediction
99: includes at least 5 correct answers (recall-5).

33: 3.4 ALCE is Robust to Shortcut Cases
34: We showcase how the ALCE evaluation is robust
35: to two possible shortcuts in §D: (1) using the top-1
36: retrieved passage as the response and citing itself,
37: and (2) using the first two sentences of the top-1
38: passage. Both cases have almost-perfect citation
39: scores, but (1) has low fluency due to its unnaturally
40: long length compared to human answers, and (2)
41: has low correctness due to low coverage.
42: 4 Modeling
43: In this section, we discuss three major modeling
44: components for an ALCE system—retrieval, syn-
45: thesis, and post-editing.
46: 4.1 Retrieval
47: We explore simple, off-the-shelf retrievers. We use
48: dense retrievers for Wikipedia, including GTR (Ni
49: et al., 2022) and DPR (Karpukhin et al., 2020);
50: we use BM25 for Sphere. For each question, we
51: retrieve the top-100 passages.
52: 4.2 Synthesis
53: We focus on how to prompt an LLM to interact
54: with the retriever, and synthesize and cite the ev-
55: idence (without fine-tuning internal parameters).
56: One noteworthy challenge is that existing LLMs
57: all have limited context window and thus can only
58: fit a handful of passages.
